How this works

  • Long-term contract assumes every gigawatt earns the same steady price, the way a signed multi-year deal would. Simple, and close to how the original model treats it.
  • Spot market is messier and more realistic. Each quarter's new capacity is sold at whatever the going rate is when it switches on, then drifts toward the long-run price over the period you choose. The rate shown is the average across everything running that quarter, weighted by size — so it can sit above or below the long-run price for a while when a lot of new, expensive capacity arrives at once.
  • Everything else — the Space and Connectivity businesses, cost ratios, depreciation, financing and tax — stays fixed at the base case on this page.
  • New spending doesn't start depreciating right away — there's a six-month lag while it gets built and switched on. After that it's written off as 70% servers over five years and 30% buildings over fourteen.
  • Cash and debt roll forward quarter by quarter. If cash would fall below a $15bn floor, the model borrows the difference at 6.5% a year. Existing debt of $39bn costs 5.5%, and cash on hand earns 4%.
  • The balance sheet is deliberately simplified: everything other than cash, property and debt is held flat, and equity is whatever makes the two sides balance. Working capital and deferred revenue aren't modelled. The memo row underneath shows how much of the change in equity is actual profit versus other items.
  • Worth being clear: this is a thought experiment, not SpaceX guidance and not an analyst forecast. Every projected figure is simply the arithmetic of the assumptions above — change them and you get a different answer.
